# Benchmarks

How long emberwave takes to decode a frame. The tables under [Results](#results) are written by `scripts/bench.sh` from its measurements; everything else on this page is written by hand.

## Reproducing

```sh
scripts/gen_bench_data.sh --noise   # the streams, once (Apple Silicon macOS: upstream's encoder)
scripts/bench.sh                    # measures, then rewrites the results below and the README summary
scripts/bench_lavapipe.sh           # optional: software Vulkan in a Linux container (needs Docker)
```

`bench.sh` keeps the raw results in `target/bench/` as JSON; `scripts/bench_report.py write target/bench BENCHMARKS.md README.md` turns them into the tables again. Quit other programs first, keep a laptop on mains power, and run it twice: the two runs should agree within a few per cent.

## What is measured

- **Streams.** Made by `scripts/gen_bench_data.sh` with upstream's encoder: 60 frames of a synthetic scene (colour bars, a gradient, a moving shaded disc, a fine grid and a little film grain) at 1080p, 1440p and 4K, 4:2:0, at 400, 700 and 1200 kB per frame, which is about 190, 340 and 580 Mbit/s at 60 frames per second. A fourth stream is 4K white noise at 3 MB per frame (1.4 Gbit/s), the worst case for coefficient decoding.
- **A frame** is what `Decoder::time_frame` does: assemble the frame from its bytes and upload it (CPU prepare), run the GPU passes and wait for them (GPU wall). `total` is the sum. The picture stays in GPU memory, as it would in a player, except in the table that says otherwise.
- **GPU pass times** come from timestamp queries written at the boundaries of the compute passes, so the five levels of the inverse transform are timed together ([`ARCHITECTURE.md`](ARCHITECTURE.md#timing)).
- **Warm-up.** At least 10 frames and at least one second are decoded first and not counted. A GPU that has been idle runs the first fraction of a second slower, which made short runs read up to three times slower (decision D19).
- **Statistics.** Mean, median and 99th percentile (nearest rank) over 600 frames, ten passes over each 60-frame stream. With only 60 frames the 99th percentile would be the slowest frame.
- **Upstream** is timed by `refgen bench` (`scripts/refgen/refgen.mm`) around upstream's C API, in the same way: give the decoder the frame's packet, encode the decode into a command buffer, commit it and wait. Its output, three 8-bit planes, stays in GPU memory, so it compares with emberwave's `--output yuv`.

## Reading the numbers

Written by hand about the v0.2.0 run of 2026-10-24; the tables above may come from a later run.

- **1.4 times upstream's time.** A 4K frame decodes to RGB in GPU memory in 1.37 ms, a twelfth of a frame at 60 Hz. To planes it takes 1.11 ms, 0.55 ms of it on the GPU, where upstream's own decoder takes 0.81 ms (0.48 ms on the GPU) at FP32 and 0.53 ms (0.24 ms) at its default FP16 on the same machine. That is 1.4 times upstream's FP32 time in total and 1.15 times on the GPU; v0.1.0 was at 5.5 and 7.6 times ([issues #11](https://github.com/with-developer/emberwave/issues/11) and [#12](https://github.com/with-developer/emberwave/issues/12), decisions D24 and D25).
- **Where the time goes.** Of the 0.82 ms of GPU time of a 4K frame to RGB, the inverse transform takes 0.40 ms, dequantisation 0.19 ms and the colour conversion 0.23 ms. The noise stream, with 2.5 times the data, costs 0.13 ms more to dequantise and 0.25 ms more on the CPU.
- **Fixed costs now matter.** Submitting the work and waiting for it adds about 0.3 ms at every size (the GPU wall-clock time minus the passes), and the CPU prepares a 4K frame in 0.23 ms, mostly uploading its 1.2 MB through `Queue::write_buffer`. At 1080p these two make up about two thirds of the 0.58 ms.
- **Reading back costs more than decoding.** Copying a 4K RGB frame to the CPU and unpacking it brings the time to 7.5 ms. A player keeps the picture on the GPU.
- **Scaling.** From 1080p to 4K the pixel count grows four times and the GPU time 3.7 times (0.22 to 0.82 ms).
- **Software rendering.** lavapipe on 16 CPU cores decodes 1080p at about 70 frames per second and 4K at about 23.
- **How firm the numbers are.** The machine was in use during the run, a screen recorder among other things, which shows mostly in the 99th percentiles: an earlier run that night put upstream's FP32 time for 4K at 0.88 ms instead of 0.81 ms. For decision D25 the versions with and without the change were measured alternately, three rounds each, and the rounds agreed within 2 %, apart from one disturbed round.
