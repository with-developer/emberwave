# Benchmarks

How long emberwave takes to decode a frame, and to encode one. The tables under [Results](#results) are written by `scripts/bench.sh` from its measurements; everything else on this page is written by hand.

## Reproducing

```sh
scripts/gen_bench_data.sh --noise   # the streams, once (Apple Silicon macOS: upstream's encoder)
scripts/gen_bench_data.sh --sources # the pictures for the encoders, once
scripts/bench.sh                    # measures, then rewrites the results below and the README summary
scripts/bench_lavapipe.sh           # optional: software Vulkan in a Linux container (needs Docker)
```

`bench.sh` keeps the raw results in `target/bench/` as JSON; `scripts/bench_report.py write target/bench BENCHMARKS.md README.md` turns them into the tables again. Quit other programs first, keep a laptop on mains power, and run it twice: the two runs should agree within a few per cent.

## What is measured

- **Streams.** Made by `scripts/gen_bench_data.sh` with upstream's encoder: 60 frames of a synthetic scene (colour bars, a gradient, a moving shaded disc, a fine grid and a little film grain) at 1080p, 1440p and 4K, 4:2:0, at 400, 700 and 1200 kB per frame, which is about 190, 340 and 580 Mbit/s at 60 frames per second. A fourth stream is 4K white noise at 3 MB per frame (1.4 Gbit/s), the worst case for coefficient decoding.
- **A frame** is what `Decoder::time_frame` does: assemble the frame from its bytes and upload it (CPU prepare), run the GPU passes and wait for them (GPU wall). `total` is the sum. The picture stays in GPU memory, as it would in a player, except in the table that says otherwise.
- **GPU pass times** come from timestamp queries written at the boundaries of the compute passes, so the five levels of the inverse transform are timed together ([`ARCHITECTURE.md`](ARCHITECTURE.md#timing)).
- **Warm-up.** At least 10 frames and at least one second are decoded first and not counted. A GPU that has been idle runs the first fraction of a second slower, which made short runs read up to three times slower (decision D19).
- **Statistics.** Mean, median and 99th percentile (nearest rank) over 600 frames, ten passes over each 60-frame stream. With only 60 frames the 99th percentile would be the slowest frame.
- **Upstream** is timed by `refgen bench` (`scripts/refgen/refgen.mm`) around upstream's C API, in the same way: give the decoder the frame's packet, encode the decode into a command buffer, commit it and wait. Its output, three 8-bit planes, stays in GPU memory, so it compares with emberwave's `--output yuv`.
- **Encoding from textures** starts from an R8 texture per plane, made from each picture before the measurement (`bench-encode --gpu --textures`, `Encoder::encode_textures`), and ends with the frame's bytes in CPU memory like the encoding from CPU memory below.
- **Encoding** goes from the picture's 8-bit planes in CPU memory to the frame's bytes in CPU memory: `Encoder::encode` for emberwave, `emberwave-cli bench-encode`; `pyrowave_encoder_encode_cpu`, counting the packets, which waits for the GPU, and `pyrowave_encoder_packetize` for upstream, `refgen bench-encode`. Both upload the planes and read the frame back within the time. The pictures are 20 frames of the same scene, made by `scripts/gen_bench_data.sh --sources`, at the budgets of the decoder's streams of the same size; both tools read them into memory first and take them in turn, so that encodes follow each other as decodes do. Upstream's encoder is timed at its default precision, an FP16 wavelet pyramid, and at FP32, which is what emberwave's uses. It hides its command buffer, so only its total is known. The CPU encoder, at a quarter of a second for a 4K frame, is timed over 60 frames instead of 600.

## Reading the numbers

Written by hand about the v0.2.0 run of 2026-10-24; the tables above may come from a later run.

- **1.4 times upstream's time.** A 4K frame decodes to RGB in GPU memory in 1.37 ms, a twelfth of a frame at 60 Hz. To planes it takes 1.11 ms, 0.55 ms of it on the GPU, where upstream's own decoder takes 0.81 ms (0.48 ms on the GPU) at FP32 and 0.53 ms (0.24 ms) at its default FP16 on the same machine. That is 1.4 times upstream's FP32 time in total and 1.15 times on the GPU; v0.1.0 was at 5.5 and 7.6 times ([issues #11](https://github.com/with-developer/emberwave/issues/11) and [#12](https://github.com/with-developer/emberwave/issues/12), decisions D24 and D25).
- **Where the time goes.** Of the 0.82 ms of GPU time of a 4K frame to RGB, the inverse transform takes 0.40 ms, dequantisation 0.19 ms and the colour conversion 0.23 ms. The noise stream, with 2.5 times the data, costs 0.13 ms more to dequantise and 0.25 ms more on the CPU.
- **Fixed costs now matter.** Submitting the work and waiting for it adds about 0.3 ms at every size (the GPU wall-clock time minus the passes), and the CPU prepares a 4K frame in 0.23 ms, mostly uploading its 1.2 MB through `Queue::write_buffer`. At 1080p these two make up about two thirds of the 0.58 ms.
- **Reading back costs about as much as decoding.** Copying a 4K RGB frame to the CPU brings the time to about 2.2 ms, 7.5 ms before decision D37 took the copies out of it. A player keeps the picture on the GPU.
- **Runs differ.** A 4K frame took 0.96 ms to planes in the runs at commit `d09a1f5`, both that one and one just before it. It took 0.87 ms at `8cd3c09` and 0.88 ms at `ff6fa28`, and it takes 0.87 to 0.89 ms measured on its own. The GPU's timestamps put the difference in the GPU's own time, 0.61 against 0.55 ms. The code of that path did not change between those commits. Upstream's FP32 time ranged from 0.76 to 0.87 ms over the same runs. Compare emberwave and upstream within one run.
- **Scaling.** From 1080p to 4K the pixel count grows four times and the GPU time 3.7 times (0.22 to 0.82 ms).
- **Software rendering.** lavapipe on 16 CPU cores decodes 1080p at about 70 frames per second and 4K at about 23.
- **How firm the numbers are.** The machine was in use during the run, a screen recorder among other things, which shows mostly in the 99th percentiles: an earlier run that night put upstream's FP32 time for 4K at 0.88 ms instead of 0.81 ms. For decision D25 the versions with and without the change were measured alternately, three rounds each, and the rounds agreed within 2 %, apart from one disturbed round.
- **The run for 0.11.0** (2026-10-26, commit `6f131a8`, the tables above). Two runs on a quiet machine agreed within 5 % on the medians, except at 4K: upstream's decoder took 0.59 and 0.54 ms at FP16 and 0.86 and 0.78 ms at FP32, and emberwave's to RGB 1.29 and 1.20 ms, the first of these disturbed, with a 99th percentile of 3.38 ms. The tables are the second run's. Next to the run at `ff6fa28`, before the textures of decisions D40 to D44 and the padded rows of the decoder's planes (D41), no mean of emberwave's moved by more than 4 %: a 4K frame takes 0.90 ms to planes instead of 0.88, 1.22 ms to RGB as before, and 1.90 ms to encode instead of 1.89. The working tree differed from the commit only in the signing team of the iOS example's Xcode project, which the measured programs do not use.
- **The fixed cost is Metal's.** Upstream's decoder shows the same gap between its wall-clock time and the span of its command buffer, 0.25 ms at 4K, as emberwave's between its wall-clock time and its passes, 0.26 ms. That is mostly committing the work and learning that it is done, which any Metal client pays, rather than wgpu.

About the encoder, written by hand about the run of 2026-10-24 with the changes of decision D30:

- **Faster than upstream's encoder.** From the planes in CPU memory to the frame in CPU memory, emberwave's GPU encoder takes 1.91 ms for a 4K frame and upstream's encoder 3.39 ms with an FP32 pyramid, as emberwave's, or 3.03 ms at its default FP16: 0.56 times its FP32 time. At 1440p it is 1.12 against 1.53 ms, at 1080p 0.82 against 0.93 ms, where fixed costs weigh more. The CPU encoder takes 218 ms for the 4K frame, 114 times the GPU's time.
- **Where the time goes.** Of the 1.91 ms at 4K, 0.23 ms is the CPU writing the planes into the upload buffer and 1.69 ms the GPU wall time: 1.31 ms of passes and the copy of the planes, the read-back of the frame and the waiting around them. Of the passes the quantisation takes 0.61 ms, the transform 0.39 ms, the packing 0.19 ms and the rate control 0.13 ms.
- **What changed.** Measured alternately with the build before D30, three rounds of 600 frames at 4K: 3.37 to 3.66 ms before and 1.88 to 1.95 ms after. The CPU's part was 1.25 to 1.28 ms, copying the planes into the new staging memory that `Queue::write_buffer` allocates for every frame, and is 0.22 to 0.23 ms; the quantisation, which added 64 squared errors one after the other per 8x8 block and number of dropped planes from workgroup memory, took 0.91 to 1.04 ms and takes 0.59 to 0.61 ms. The planes now go into an upload buffer the encoder keeps from frame to frame, and each invocation adds up its own subblock's errors in registers.
- **From textures** (the run at commit `991f541`, decision D32): a 4K frame takes 1.80 ms from an R8 texture per plane against 1.93 ms from CPU memory. The CPU's part falls from 0.23 to 0.02 ms, but the pass that packs the textures into the planes makes the transform 0.12 ms longer, more than the copy from the upload buffer it replaces; reading the textures in the transform itself would save that ([#40](https://github.com/with-developer/emberwave/issues/40)). At 1080p both take 0.80 ms. A screen capture that would otherwise come back to CPU memory saves its read-back as well.
- **Upstream** copies the planes once, into textures it keeps from frame to frame (`replaceRegion`), as emberwave copies them once into its upload buffer, whose contents the GPU then copies again into its planes buffer. Upstream also has an entry point that encodes from pictures already in GPU memory (`pyrowave_encoder_encode_gpu`), which these numbers do not use; emberwave's, `Encoder::encode_textures` ([#33](https://github.com/with-developer/emberwave/issues/33)), has a table of its own below.
- **How firm the numbers are.** Upstream's FP32 time for 4K was 3.18 ms in the run before this one and 3.39 ms in this one, emberwave's 1.88 and 1.91 ms: the comparison holds either way. A first run read the pictures from the file between the encodes, which made emberwave's encode slower and upstream's hardly at all: a GPU that waits for the CPU slows down, and not every GPU alike. Both tools read the pictures into memory first, as the decoder benchmarks have their streams.

## In the browser, phones included

[`bench.html`](https://with-developer.github.io/emberwave/bench.html), next to the browser player, measures on whatever device opens it: it encodes test streams at the three sizes in the page, four pictures each at the budgets above, and decodes them in turn to RGB in GPU memory through WebGPU, waiting for each frame, 300 frames after at least 30 and a second of warm-up ([`ARCHITECTURE.md`](ARCHITECTURE.md#browser-player)). The times include what the browser adds to the GPU work: its checks, its process boundary and the delay until it reports the frame done. Browsers coarsen their clocks, Safari to a millisecond, so the mean is taken over the whole run.

Mean milliseconds per frame, 2026-10-24:

| Device | Browser | 1080p | 1440p | 4K | CPU part at 4K |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| iPhone 17 | Safari 27.0.1 | 2.36 | 3.90 | 8.05 | 0.26 |
| Apple M3 Max | Chrome 155, headless, four runs | 0.70 to 1.00 | 0.98 to 1.04 | 1.69 to 1.88 | 0.56 |
| Apple M3 Max, natively | `emberwave-cli bench --output rgb`, the run of 2026-10-24 | 0.52 | 0.68 | 1.19 | 0.10 |

- **On the iPhone 17** a 4K frame decodes in about 8 ms, under half the 16.7 ms of a frame at 60 Hz, and a 1080p frame in 2.4 ms. The CPU's part is small, 0.11 to 0.26 ms; the rest is the GPU and the browser's report. Safari's per-frame medians and 99th percentiles, 2, 4 and 8 ms and 3, 5 and 9 ms, are as coarse as its clock. Its user agent reads "iPhone OS 18_7".
- **Next to the M3 Max** in Chrome the phone takes 2.4 to 4.8 times as long, more at 4K than at 1080p; the first size of a run on the Mac varied most between runs. A native app does not pay the browser's share, which the M3 Max shows at 0.2 to 0.7 ms a frame, and the iPhone 17 at 0.7 to 1.8 ms ([below](#on-the-iphone-natively)).

## On the iPhone, natively

The benchmark app, [`crates/emberwave-ffi/examples/ios`](crates/emberwave-ffi/examples/ios), times emberwave through the XCFramework's C API, as an app that uses it would, with the pictures and budgets of `bench.html`. Each number is over 300 frames after at least 30 and a second of warm-up, four pictures in turn, and runs until the call returns with the GPU done. On the app's queue, where the call does not wait, it runs until the app's next command buffer, a copy of one pixel, has read the picture.

Measured on 2026-10-26 on an iPhone 17 (`iPhone18,3`, the A19's GPU through Metal) with iOS 27.0.1 (24A446), with the XCFramework of release 0.11.0, a Release build started from the home screen, without a debugger. There were two runs 12 minutes apart. The thermal state was nominal at the end of both, and Low Power Mode was off. Mean milliseconds per frame, the two runs:

| Way | 1080p, 400 kB | 1440p, 700 kB | 4K, 1200 kB |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decode into a Metal texture (RGB) | 1.62 to 1.63 | 2.73 to 2.74 | 6.21 to 6.29 |
| Decode into Y, Cb and Cr textures | 1.27 to 1.29 | 2.11 to 2.13 | 4.78 to 4.83 |
| Decode on the app's queue, until its read | 1.64 to 1.66 | 2.81 to 2.82 | 6.51 to 6.58 |
| Decode to planes in CPU memory | 1.32 | 2.15 to 2.23 | 4.89 to 4.96 |
| Encode from CPU memory | 2.23 to 2.29 | 3.82 to 3.83 | 8.06 to 8.29 |

The 99th percentiles were within 10 % of the means.

- **Checked on the phone's GPU.** The app's three checks passed on the A19's GPU:
  - the picture read back through Metal equals the RGB decode;
  - the planes equal those decoded to memory;
  - a decode on the app's queue, read by the app's next command buffer, equals the RGB decode.

  It was the first run of the XCFramework on an iPhone's own GPU. CI runs the app in the iOS Simulator, which uses the Mac's GPU.
- **Without the browser.** Into a texture, a frame takes 22 to 31 % less than in Safari on the same phone: 1.62 against 2.36 ms at 1080p, 2.73 against 3.90 ms at 1440p and 6.21 to 6.29 against 8.05 ms at 4K.
- **The planes are the quickest way to the GPU here too**, 20 to 23 % under the RGB texture. A player that converts the colours in its own draw saves that much.
- **Next to the M3 Max** through the C API (decisions D41 and D42), the phone takes about 3 times as long at 1080p and 4.5 to 4.8 times at 4K.
- **Within a frame.** The iPhone 17 refreshes its display at up to 120 Hz, 8.3 ms a frame. A 1080p frame takes a fifth of that into a texture, and a 4K frame 58 % of it into the planes.
- **Memory.** The planes in CPU memory cost about what the plane textures do, as the phone's CPU and GPU share their memory.
- **Upstream's decoder** has not been timed on the phone: `refgen` runs on macOS only.
